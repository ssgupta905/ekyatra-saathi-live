THREE RULES OF DELIVERY
  1. Say the number, then say what it means. Never a number on its own.
  2. Never say "stops", "fails" or "blocked" without, in the same breath,
     saying what happened next. A demo that describes a failure and pauses
     has shown the audience a broken product.
  3. When a verdict changes on screen, STOP TALKING and let it change. That
     silence is the most persuasive second in the whole demo.

PART 1 — THE OPENING                                        (no clip · 60 sec)
================================================================================

   A family in Bihar travels six hundred kilometres to a cancer hospital in
   Mumbai. They arrive. The report isn't back. So they go home, and they come
   again.

   Sixty per cent of that hospital's patients come from outside the state.
   Three quarters travel more than five hundred kilometres. Eighty-three per
   cent of them hit catastrophic non-medical costs — a mean of eighty-eight
   thousand rupees. More than half borrow money or sell something to cope.

   The treatment is often free. It is the journey that ruins them.

   And the reason is structural. Every department optimises its own queue —
   the lab, the scheme desk, the pharmacy, the cab firm, the hotel. Each one
   is efficient on its own terms. Nobody counts the patient's trips.

   EKYATRA Saathi is the layer that counts them.

   Two things it is not.

   It is not a hospital management system. It stores no clinical record and
   it replaces nothing. It reads status out of whatever the hospital already
   runs, decides whether the next visit can actually happen, and tells the
   people who need to know.

   And it is not only for cancer. A care pathway is declared as a graph of
   steps and the dependencies between them. Three ship today: oncology,
   maintenance dialysis, and recovery after discharge. The decision core
   never knows which one it is working on. Adding a condition is a
   declaration, not a rewrite.

   Srinivas Pavan, delivery manager — four weeks of planning, and the scope
   calls that kept it shippable.

   Our doctor partner is Doctor Arani Chakraborty. M.B.B.S., registration
   nine three six nine eight. Neurology at Manipal Hospital, E M Bypass;
   formerly neurosurgery at AIIMS Kalyani.

   His contribution changed the shape of the product. He mapped the sequence
   a patient actually moves through — presentation, evaluation, the wait for
   a result, follow-up — and told us where that sequence breaks: the points
   at which a patient is sent away and does not come back.

   That is why a journey here is modelled as a chain with one thing blocking
   it, rather than a calendar of appointments. And why we withhold the date
   until the chain clears.

   To be precise about the limits: this is clinical process review. It is not
   a clinical trial, and no real patient data has been used anywhere.

PART 3 — HOW THE APPROACH CHANGED                           (no clip · 30 sec)
================================================================================

   We did not start here.

   The idea we submitted was a reminder app. Tell the family their
   appointment is coming.

   Within the first week it was obvious the useful question is the opposite
   one. Not "when is your appointment" — but "should you come at all?"

   So we rebuilt around a readiness verdict. The hospital says come back in a
   week. Saathi asks whether the thing you are coming for will actually be
   ready, and only then gives you a date.

PART 4 — THE ARCHITECTURE                                   (no clip · 60 sec)
================================================================================
Show the "Where it sits" diagram from the presentation, or clip 01 at [0:51].

   Three layers.

   Above the line: what the hospital already runs. Its own system for
   appointments and report status. ABDM, for linked status under India's own
   consent model. The care pathways themselves. And the directory of labs,
   cabs, hotels, pharmacies and scheme desks it already uses.

   Saathi reads from all of those. It never writes anything clinical.

   In the middle: the orchestration layer. It resolves the care pathway as a
   dependency graph. It forecasts when each pending step will be ready. It
   asks every party the journey leans on. It reaches a verdict. And it writes
   every fact it learns into a hash-chained ledger.

   Below the line: three audiences, each seeing only what they need.
   The navigator sees every journey against whatever is holding it up.
   The family sees one word and one date, in their own language.
   Each vendor sees one job, and nothing at all about anybody's care.

   On the technology: the decision itself uses no model. It is a dependency
   graph, split conformal prediction for the readiness forecast with a stated
   coverage level, change-point detection for drop-off, and the ledger.

   An agentic AI layer does one job — putting a decided plan into the
   family's own language. It never decides anything. Sarvam provides speech
   in Hindi, Bengali, Odia and English.

   Underneath: FastAPI, PostgreSQL, React, Kotlin — and six hundred and
   thirty-one automated tests that run with no credentials at all.

   in Patna. Meena in Varanasi.

   And note Meena is on dialysis — not cancer. Same system, different

   Every journey against every party holding it up.

[0:36]  MEENA — the system working normally
   Meena's verdict is Wait. The visit depends on something not settled yet.

   Saathi asks her cab firm. The cab firm answers for itself. Nothing here
   was typed by a person.

[1:00]  ** STOP TALKING. LET IT CHANGE. **
   Wait becomes Come. A date appears.

   The date arrived because the chain cleared. It is never a guess, and it is
   never shown early.

[1:31]  RAVI — the most important ninety seconds of the demo
   Now Ravi. His cab firm comes back and says it cannot do the journey.

   ** Say this exactly: **

   Here is the whole point of the product. Saathi caught that this morning —
   before Ravi left home. Under the old way, he finds out at the station, or
   when the car simply never arrives.

   So Saathi does not invent a date it cannot honour. It does not quietly
   substitute another vendor behind the scenes. It says plainly which party
   failed, and it waits for a person — because choosing who to use is a human
   decision, not an algorithm's.

[1:50]  The recovery
   The navigator presses "Book another". A second firm, Ola, is asked.

   The system did not choose that vendor. A person did, from the directory.
   And the ledger records who asked, which firm, and when.

[2:50]  Ravi is fine
   Ola says ready. Everything else was ready already — so Ravi goes straight
   back to Come, with a date.

   One vendor failing cost a navigator a few minutes. It did not cost Ravi a
   wasted six-hundred-kilometre journey. He never knew anything went wrong.

[3:06]  ANITA — one click
   Now Anita. Her report is still sitting with the lab. That is the single
   thing her entire journey is waiting on.

   The lab desk opens Releases and marks it released. One click, by one
   person, and that is the only thing they do.

[3:18]  The chain fills in
   That one fact unlocks everything behind it. Saathi now asks each remaining
   party in turn — the insurer, the pharmacy, the ride, the stay — the way a
   navigator would, except in seconds rather than across a morning of calls.

   And the family's phone updates by itself. Nobody sends them anything.

[4:26]  Done
   The chain is complete. All three families are ready to travel.

   Two of them began this morning held up — one by a lab, one by a cab firm —
   and neither family had to telephone anybody to find that out.

CLIP 06 — AFFORDABILITY                                             (1:04)
================================================================================
This is the differentiator. Do not rush it.

[0:00]
   The planner can work out that this journey needs a ride and a bed. It
   cannot work out whether this household can pay for the ones it picked.

   Not every family can absorb an app cab and a hotel. Eighty-three per cent
   of them are already in catastrophic costs.

[0:12]
   So Saathi asks. And every option on this screen is a real organisation
   from the hospital's own directory — a railway patient-quota berth, a
   shared jeep, a taxi.

   This family's ration card is AAY, so the concession is offered first, and
   marked. It is not pre-selected. A default nobody changed is not an answer
   to "what can you manage".

[0:28]
   There is no price anywhere on this screen. Saathi doesn't have one, and a
   number invented for a screen would be worse than silence.

[0:35]  ** the line to land **
   And last on every list — deliberately not one of the things to buy:
   "I cannot manage any of these."

   That answer does not book a cheaper cab. It raises a social worker.
   Because the answer to a family with no fare is a person and a scheme.

[0:45]

   then does the ride move — from a taxi, to the railway desk — on the

PART 5 — SAFETY AND RESPONSIBLE AI                          (no clip · 60 sec)
================================================================================

   Four things make this safe to put in front of a family.

   One. The model never decides whether a family travels. A deterministic
   planner decides; the model only puts that decision into their language.
   The response schema's verdict list is derived from the code's own decision
   type, so a new verdict cannot silently fall outside it.

   Two. Report content never enters the system. The ingest drops conclusion,
   result and presented-form. The record pointer has no field that could hold
   a finding. Saathi cannot leak what it is structurally unable to store.

   Three. A clinical question is never answered by software. A guard screens
   every message in both directions and routes it to a human. A diverted
   message reaches the family as "a person is calling you" — never as blank
   words.

   Four. Honest uncertainty. There is no score anywhere in this system. A
   forecast carries its coverage level, and below a hundred and twenty
   calibration observations it declines to answer at all — the verdict
   becomes Uncertain and a person is told. "We don't know yet" costs a family
   nothing. A date we invented costs them a journey.

   And on fairness: there is deliberately no stored field for how poor a
   family is. Only a household whose own papers say so is offered a
   subsidised bed first — because a charity bed taken by someone who could
   pay for a lodge is a bed a destitute family did not get.

PART 6 — DOES IT WORK                                       (no clip · 45 sec)
================================================================================

   We scored it against planted ground truth.

   Fifty synthetic families. For each one we generate the true date their
   pending report actually lands. Then we ask the only question that matters:
   if the family had travelled on the date they were given, would the step
   have been possible?

   The hospital's default policy produces thirty wasted journeys out of
   fifty. Sixty per cent.

   Saathi produces none.

   At the published figure of eight and a half thousand rupees per journey,
   that is two lakh fifty-five thousand rupees of household cost avoided,
   across fifty families.

   Be clear about what that is. These are synthetic families with planted
   answers, not patients. The turnaround distributions come from published
   queue behaviour, not one hospital's real data. No caregiver has used this,
   and no clinician has used the console in a live clinic.

   Replacing that number with a real one is exactly what the pilot is for.


================================================================================
PART 7 — DEPLOYMENT                                         (no clip · 40 sec)
================================================================================

   What a pilot needs: a named hospital partner, and an Institutional Ethics
   Committee approval. Before any real-data pilot, not after.

   Integration happens at three seams, and all three are thin. ABDM OAuth and
   consent-manager routing — specified; the consent model is built and
   enforced, the live handshake is not yet wired. One read-only feed of
   appointment and report status from the hospital's existing system. And the
   directory of vendors it already uses.

   Saathi reads status and writes nothing clinical. That is the reason this
   can be piloted in one quarter rather than becoming an HMIS project.

   Scaling is linear in families, not hospitals. A second centre is
   configuration — its own pathway rules, its own directory — not a fork.

PART 8 — THE BUSINESS MODEL                                 (no clip · 75 sec)
================================================================================
>>> IMPORTANT: the pricing below is a PROPOSAL, not a decided model. Every
>>> number is marked. Agree these with the team before presenting, or present
>>> the structure and say pricing is being validated in the pilot.

   Who pays. Not the family. The family is the beneficiary, and a family
   already in catastrophic costs cannot be the revenue line.

   The payer is whoever currently absorbs the cost of a wasted visit. That is
   the hospital, and behind the hospital, the scheme.

   Why the hospital pays. A patient who arrives for a step that cannot happen
   occupies a slot in a queue that is already the bottleneck. A no-show after
   a wasted trip does the same. Reducing wasted arrivals gives the hospital
   back capacity it is currently losing — at a tertiary centre seeing
   forty-five thousand new patients a year, that is material.

   Why the scheme pays. PM-JAY and the state schemes carry the downstream
   cost of patients who disengage from treatment and return later, sicker.
   Follow-up loss is expensive to the payer long before it is visible.

   The unit. We price per navigation desk per month, because that is the
   operational unit a hospital actually budgets — one desk serving roughly
   five hundred active families.

   The numbers.  [ALL FIGURES BELOW ARE PROPOSED — CONFIRM BEFORE USE]
   Our cost to run one desk is four to nine thousand rupees a month:
   infrastructure, plus three to eight rupees per active family for model and
   speech calls.
   Proposed price: twenty-five to fifty thousand rupees per desk per month.
   Which is between three and six avoided journeys, out of a desk that should
   be avoiding dozens.

   One-time deployment: six to nine person-weeks for the ABDM wiring, the
   status-feed adapter, and configuration. Roughly one point eight to two
   point seven lakh at a blended rate.

   Why the economics hold. The marginal cost of planning one family for a
   month is a few rupees. One avoided journey is eight and a half thousand.
   That conclusion survives being wrong about our cost by two orders of
   magnitude.

   Three channels, in the order we would pursue them.
   One: a paid pilot at a single tertiary centre, on institutional or grant
   support, to produce the real number.
   Two: public-sector procurement through the scheme, where avoided wasted
   utilisation is the buyer's own metric.
   Three: CSR and philanthropic funding, specifically to underwrite the
   subsidised tier — the dharamshala beds and the patient-quota berths — so
   affordability routing has somewhere to route to.

   What we are not doing: charging families, selling data, or taking a
   commission from vendors. A commission would corrupt the one thing this
   product must be trusted on — which vendor a family is sent to, and why.

PART 9 — WHAT NEXT, AND THE CLOSE                           (no clip · 45 sec)
================================================================================

   The plan, in order.

   One: a named hospital partner and an ethics approval. Everything waits on
   this. A draft inquiry to ICMR-NCDIR is written and unsent.
   Two: finish the ABDM handshake, so status arrives under real consent.
   Three: a single-desk pilot — fifty to a hundred families, one quarter. The
   primary endpoint is the one this was built for: wasted journeys per family
   per quarter, against that desk's own prior practice.
   Four: replace the synthetic calibration with the hospital's own turnaround
   data, and the published per-trip cost with measured spend.
   Five: close the known gaps — the budget conversation on Android, the
   nearer-treatment-centre option, and an IVR path for families without a
   smartphone.

   THE CLOSE

   Three rules hold all of this up.

   The model never decides whether a family travels.
   No report content ever enters the system.
   And every decision sits in a log that cannot be quietly edited.

   Everything you have seen runs against a deployed service, with real
   records and a real phone. The console reflects the world. It never causes
   it.

   Thank you.
