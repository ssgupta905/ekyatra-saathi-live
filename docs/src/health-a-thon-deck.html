Problem Statement and Supporting Evidence

A family travels 600 km for an appointment and arrives to find the report is not back.

The visit is wasted. The journey is not — it is paid for in fares, lodging, and days not earning. Then they go home and do it again.

Why it matters

Tata Memorial Hospital patients from outside Maharashtra60%
Patients studied who travelled over 500 km75%+
Facing catastrophic non-medical costs (mean ₹88,433)83%
Borrowing or selling assets to cope, over 500 km53%
Share of that cost that is travel and accommodation76%

Tata Memorial Centre annual figures; IIPS / TMC study, Journal of Cancer Policy, 2025.

What is wrong today

  • Every department optimises its own queue. Pathology, radiology, the scheme desk and the pharmacy each run their own backlog. Nobody counts the family's trips.
  • The return date is a policy, not a fact. "Come back in a week" is issued before anyone knows when the report lands — and pathology is both slower and far more variable than radiology, so one rule strands people.
  • The caregiver carries the integration. One family member chases the lab, the insurer, the cab and the room, usually by phone, often in a language the hospital does not use.
  • Nothing is told to the family between visits. A delay inside the hospital is invisible until they have already travelled.
  • Follow-up loss is discovered late. By the time a patient is noticed missing, the window has often closed.

What needs to improve

Replace a fixed return date with a readiness decision: do not ask a family to travel for a step that is not ready, and tell them, in their own language, the moment it is.

Target Users and Use Case

The caregiver

Primary user

Usually the one family member holding the whole journey together. Reads Hindi, Bengali or Odia more comfortably than English; may not read at all. A basic Android phone on an intermittent connection.

Needs: one word — come or wait — one date, what to carry, and which desk to reach first. Nothing else.

Fits their workflow by replacing a phone call they would otherwise make to the hospital, and by arriving before they set out rather than after.

The patient navigator

Primary user, hospital side

Holds around thirty families at once at a tertiary centre's navigation desk. Today works from a list sorted by date.

Needs: to see which journeys are blocked and by whom, not which are due.

Fits their workflow as one board replacing thirty phone calls: every family against every party that holds them up, worst first. They still make every decision — the system only asks the questions.

The desks and vendors

Secondary users

The lab release desk, the scheme desk, a cab firm, a hotel, a pharmacy.

Needs: to answer one question about one job, without seeing a patient record.

Fits their workflow as a single screen showing only their own organisation's jobs — a first name, a district, a day — and a phone number only when the family has agreed to share it.

Setting: a tertiary cancer centre's patient-navigation desk and the homes of the families it serves, typically 400–1,900 km away. The task: decide, every day, for every family, whether the next hospital visit is worth making — and act on the answer.

Solution Overview, Key Features & Impact (1 of 2)

Saathi answers one question, every day, for every family: is this trip worth making?

It reads appointment and report status from ABDM and hospital feeds — never report content — resolves the care pathway's dependency graph, forecasts when each pending step will be ready, and returns one of four verdicts. A date appears only when every prerequisite is satisfied, so the app never promises a day it does not have.

आइए  Come

The chain is clear. A date, what to bring, and the first desk to reach.

रुकिए  Wait

Something is still blocking it. Who has it, and the forecast window — never a promised date.

Re-plan

Something broke. Options are ranked for a person to choose; treatment timing needs a doctor.

Uncertain is the fourth, and the one most systems leave out. When calibration is too thin to forecast honestly, Saathi says so and tells a navigator, rather than guessing.

End-to-end journey

Input What Saathi does Output
Appointment and report status; care steps; the family's language, district and declared circumstances Resolves the dependency chain → forecasts readiness with a stated coverage level → asks every party the journey leans on → when a family can go, asks what they can afford → renders the decision into their language A verdict and, when earned, a date, a bring-list and a first stop — on the family's phone, read aloud on request; and a board of blocked journeys for the navigator

Solution Overview, Key Features & Impact (2 of 2)

Key features

  • A readiness verdict, not a reminder. Four answers, a date only when the chain is clear, and a forecast that carries its own coverage level instead of a confident guess.
  • The whole network, asked automatically. Scheme desk, pharmacy, cab firm, hotel and lab each answer for themselves. A vendor signs in to its own screen and sees only its own jobs.
  • Affordability built into the plan. A railway patient-quota berth and a dharamshala for one household, an app cab and a hotel for another — and Saathi asks the family to confirm, with "I cannot manage any of these" always on the list, routed to a social worker rather than a cheaper booking.
  • Four languages, and a voice. Hindi, Bengali, Odia, English, each read aloud on request. A plan a caregiver cannot read is not a plan.
  • An audit trail that cannot be quietly edited. A hash-chained ledger that grows only when a fact actually changes.

Expected impact

Measured against planted ground truth on 50 synthetic families (python -m saathi.demo):

Wasted trips under the hospital's default policy30 / 50  (60%)
Wasted trips with Saathi0 / 50  (0%)
Non-medical cost avoided, at ₹8,500 per trip₹2,55,000

Synthetic, not clinical. The point of the number is the mechanism it demonstrates, not the magnitude.

If it held at a single tertiary centre with ~45,000 new patients a year, of whom 60% travel from outside the state, each avoided return journey is roughly a month's income for a household in the lowest band — alongside a clinical gain that is harder to count: a family who is not exhausted by a wasted trip is a family more likely to come back for the next one.

And for the hospital: fewer patients arriving for a step that cannot happen means fewer wasted slots in queues that are already the bottleneck.

Technology, Tools and AI Approach

The model never decides whether a family travels. A deterministic planner decides. The language model is given one job — putting that decision into the family's own language — and is bound by a response schema whose verdict enum is derived from the Decision type itself, so a new verdict cannot silently fall outside it.

Decision core — no model

  • Dependency-graph resolution over the care pathway
  • Split conformal prediction for readiness intervals, with a stated coverage level (90%, n=120 per queue) that refuses to answer on thin calibration
  • PELT change-point detection for engagement drop-off
  • Hash-chained evidence ledger

Where AI is used

  • Rendering — an agentic AI layer turns a decided plan into a sentence in the family's language. A 20B-class model, deliberately small.
  • Reading a reply — an inbound message becomes a proposal a navigator confirms, never an instruction
  • Speech — Sarvam bulbul:v3 and saarika:v2.5 for Hindi, Bengali, Odia, English, with a general-purpose model as a fallback ear
  • Simulated counterparties — gpt-oss-120b stands in for vendors in the demo, never in a decision

Platform & integrations

  • Python 3.12 · FastAPI · Pydantic v2 · LangGraph
  • PostgreSQL with Alembic migrations
  • React 19 + Vite — four applications
  • Kotlin + Jetpack Compose — Android
  • Server-sent events; Docker on Render
  • ABDM status + consent model; hospital appointment/report feed; a directory of the vendors a journey already uses

Sarvam was chosen for a concrete reason: almost no phone ships an Odia voice, so read-aloud silently did nothing for Odia families.

How it evolved from the idea submission

The idea was a reminder app: tell the family their appointment is coming. Within the first week it was clear the useful question is the opposite one — should they come at all — and the system was rebuilt around a readiness verdict. The second change came from the clinical review: model the journey as a dependency chain with a blocking step, not a calendar.

Now functional

Four-verdict planner with calibrated forecasts · hospital control centre (15 sections) · family web and Android apps in four languages with Sarvam speech · partner app for vendors · seven-stage onboarding · consent · hash-chained audit · means-aware planning and the budget conversation · deployed and running.

Major challenges, and what changed

  • One screen for two audiences served neither. Split into separate applications in week 3 — the single largest rework of the sprint.
  • A rendering enum had rotted and was silently diverting every re-plan message. Found by driving the deployment, not the test suite; the enum is now derived from the type and pinned by a test.
  • 110 model calls and a 226-second stall on first load. Reduced to 9 calls and 43 seconds by skipping a first-pass render, excluding seed data and serialising under the rate limit.
  • One directory of vendors could not serve every family. Offering the same app cab and hotel to a household on a daily wage and one on a salary is not one plan, so the directory gained cost tiers and the planner learned to read them.

Deliberately incomplete

ABDM OAuth and consent-manager routing — the consent model is built and enforced; the live ABDM handshake is specified and not wired. The budget conversation on Android — built for web, strings generated for Android, screen not yet built. Nearer-treatment-centre option — modelled, not surfaced. Each is named in the README rather than left to be discovered.

Evaluation and User Validation

No real patient data has been used anywhere, and there has been no patient-facing user testing. Everything below is measured on synthetic data or on the running system. Saying otherwise would be the easiest claim in this deck to get wrong.

How it was tested

Planted ground truth. 50 synthetic families are generated together with the true date each pending report actually lands. That lets us ask the only question that matters: if the family had travelled on the date they were given, would the step have been possible?

MetricResult

Evidence ledger intact after the runyes
Automated tests, all credential-free631 passing

Model performance

The forecast is a split conformal interval with a guaranteed coverage level rather than a point estimate: at α=0.1 the true readiness date falls inside the quoted window at least 90% of the time under exchangeability, and below 120 calibration observations the predictor declines to answer and the verdict becomes Uncertain. Rendering is checked not for fluency but for obedience — the verdict in the message must equal the planner's verdict, enforced by schema and test.

Review, findings and what changed

  • Clinical process review (Dr Chakraborty). Finding: patients drop out at the wait-for-result and follow-up steps, not at booking. Change: the verdict is withheld until the blocking step clears, and follow-up loss became an alert a navigator receives.
  • Driving the live deployment rather than the test suite. Findings: a rotted enum diverting every re-plan message; a 226-second cold start; a confirmation endpoint returning 500 on the exact configuration the demo uses. Change: all three fixed and each pinned by a regression test.
  • Reading the recordings frame by frame. Finding: a walkthrough showed an empty-state screen that was no longer true. Change: cut, and the real screen recorded.
  • Language check. Finding: read-aloud did nothing at all, silently, for Odia. Change: Sarvam replaced the browser voice on web and Android.

Limitations — what still needs validating

Synthetic turnaround distributions are drawn from published queue behaviour, not one hospital's real data. The per-trip cost is a share of a published mean, not measured spend. No caregiver has used this. No clinician has used the console in a live clinic. All four need a hospital partner and an IEC approval — that is the next step, not a footnote.

Safety and Responsible AI

Data privacy, consent and security

  • Report content never enters the system. The ABDM ingest drops conclusion, result and presentedForm, and the pointer type has no field that could hold a finding. Saathi cannot leak what it is structurally unable to store.
  • Consent per purpose, withdrawable, checked at every read and logged with refusals (withdrawn, expired, outside what was agreed, never asked). Saathi is the requester, never the consent manager. DPDP Act 2023 / ABDM model.
  • Need-to-know by role. A vendor sees a first name, a district and a day — and a phone number only if the family agreed. A navigator sees a record's category, not the test name. Self-reported medical background is visible to doctors and administrators only.
  • Tamper-evident audit. Every fact and decision is a row in a hash-chained ledger that grows only when something actually changes.

AI limitations and how failure is handled

  • The model cannot decide. It renders a decision already made. The schema binds it to the planner's verdict.
  • Thin data ⇒ no answer. Below 120 calibration observations the forecaster declines; the verdict becomes Uncertain and a person is told. "We don't know yet" costs a family nothing; a date we invented costs them a journey.
  • A clinical question is never answered. The guard routes it to a human; a diverted message reaches the family as "a person is calling you", never as blank words.
  • A refusal is a result. A speech or routing service that fails returns nothing rather than a plausible guess, and the fallback is visible on the health page — which now lists who is asked, because a key that is set is not a key that works.

Oversight, bias, accessibility

  • Human in the loop where it matters: a doctor for treatment timing, a person for vendor choice, a navigator before a family's budget answer takes effect.
  • No scores anywhere. No composite number that could be sorted by — and deliberately no stored field for how poor a family is.
  • Rationing is protected. Only a household whose own papers say so is offered a subsidised bed first; a charity bed taken by someone who could pay is one a destitute family did not get.
  • Accessibility is the premise. Four languages, read aloud, on a basic phone, over 2G — because the families least able to absorb a wasted trip are exactly the ones least served by English-only software.

Deployment, Cost and Sustainability Plan

What a pilot requires

  • A named hospital partner and an Institutional Ethics Committee approval — before any real-data pilot, not after
  • One navigation desk and its existing vendor relationships
  • Read access to appointment and report status; no write access to anything clinical

Integration — yes, at three seams

  • ABDM — OAuth and consent-manager routing for linked status (specified; the consent model is built and enforced, the live handshake is not yet wired)
  • Hospital HMIS / LIS — one read-only feed of appointment and report status. Saathi reads status and writes nothing clinical, which is the thinnest seam available and the reason this can be piloted without an HMIS project.
  • Directory — the labs, pharmacies, cab firms, hotels and scheme desks the hospital already uses; each answers for itself through its own screen

Scaling and sustaining

The decision core is stateless per family and the cost grows linearly with families, not with hospitals. A second centre is configuration — its own pathway rules, its own directory — not a fork. Deployable inside a hospital's own network with no change to the decision core.

Sustaining it: the natural payer is the hospital or the scheme that already absorbs the cost of a wasted slot, not the family. The cheapest-adequate models are used deliberately (a 20B model for rendering), rate-limited and serialised.

What Next? Plans to take the initiative forward

The plan, in order

  • 1 · A named hospital partner and an IEC approval. Everything else waits on this. A draft validation inquiry to ICMR-NCDIR is written and unsent; the realistic route is through a partner hospital's own registry and research office rather than a cold approach.
  • 2 · Finish the ABDM handshake. OAuth and consent-manager routing, so status arrives under real consent rather than a configured feed.
  • 3 · A single-desk pilot, 50–100 families, one quarter. The primary endpoint is the one this was built for: wasted journeys per family per quarter, against the desk's own prior practice. Secondary: follow-up loss, and time the navigator spends on the phone.
  • 4 · Replace the synthetic calibration with the hospital's own turnaround data, and the published per-trip cost with measured spend.
  • 5 · Close the known gaps — the budget conversation on Android, the nearer-treatment-centre option, IVR for families without a smartphone.

Funding

Not raised and not currently sought. The pilot above is small enough to run on grant or institutional support; the sensible long-term payer is the hospital or scheme that already absorbs the cost of a wasted slot, not the family.

Make the solution information publicly available?

Yes — we are willing for the solution information to be shared on Health-a-thon or other websites.

The architecture, the nine invariants and the evaluation method are all things we would rather see copied than kept, and the repository is already public. Nothing about a real patient exists to share in any case — the demo runs on synthetic families throughout.
