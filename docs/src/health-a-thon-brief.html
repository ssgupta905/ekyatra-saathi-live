About the Team / Organisation

A. Two independent engineers, not a registered organisation. Siddhartha Sengupta and Ankshuk Ray built Saathi over the sprint as a working system rather than a prototype shell: 160 Python modules, a React monorepo of four apps, a Kotlin/Compose Android app, and 631 automated tests that run with no credentials at all. Both come from production software engineering, which is why every safety property below is enforced by a test rather than by convention.

B. Doctor background and role in the solution.

The team has no clinician member, and we would rather say so than imply otherwise. Saathi is deliberately a non-clinical logistics tool, and the clinician role is designed into the system rather than performed by the team: a re-plan touching treatment or dose timing can be approved only by a doctor (a server-side check, not a UI rule); any message reading as a clinical question is routed to a human and never answered; the family's self-reported medical background is visible to doctors and administrators only. Clinical validation is the explicit next step — a draft inquiry to ICMR-NCDIR sits in the repository, unsent. If the team has a clinical advisor, their background and role belong here.

Problem Statement (use case specific)

A family travels hundreds of kilometres for a hospital appointment and arrives to find the report is not back, the doctor is not free, or the cab never came. The visit is wasted; the journey is not.

Tata Memorial Hospital patients from outside Maharashtra60%
Patients studied who travelled over 500 km75%+
Facing catastrophic non-medical costs (mean ₹88,433)83%
Borrowing or selling assets to cope, over 500 km53%

Sources: Tata Memorial Centre annual figures; IIPS / TMC study, Journal of Cancer Policy, 2025.

Target user: the caregiver — usually the one family member holding the whole journey together, often reading Hindi, Bengali or Odia rather than English, on a basic Android phone. Secondary user: the hospital's patient navigator, holding thirty such families at once. Every department optimises its own queue; nobody counts the family's trips.

AI Solution Description

Saathi answers one question, every day, for every family: is this trip worth making? It reads appointment and report status from ABDM and hospital feeds — never report content — resolves a dependency graph of the care pathway, forecasts when each pending step will be ready, and returns one of four verdicts: Come, Wait, Re-plan or Uncertain. A date appears only when every prerequisite is satisfied, so the app never promises a day it does not have.

Two surfaces share a backend and nothing else. The caregiver gets one word, one date, what to bring and where to go first, in their own language and read aloud on request. The navigator gets a board of every journey against every party holding it up — lab, insurer, pharmacy, cab, hotel — and acts on the stuck ones.

The decision is not made by a model. A deterministic planner decides; the model only renders that decision into the family's language, bound by a schema whose verdict enum is derived from the Decision type so it cannot drift. A guard screens every message in both directions.

Technology and Tools Used

Decision core (no model): dependency-graph resolution over the care pathway; split conformal prediction for readiness intervals with a stated coverage level (90%, n=120 per queue) that refuses to answer on thin calibration; PELT change-point detection for engagement drop-off; a hash-chained ledger.

Language & speech: Groq gpt-oss-20b (rendering) and gpt-oss-120b (simulated counterparties); Sarvam bulbul:v3 TTS and saarika:v2.5 STT for Hindi, Bengali, Odia and English, with whisper-large-v3-turbo as a fallback ear. Sarvam was chosen for a concrete reason: almost no phone ships an Odia voice, so read-aloud silently did nothing for Odia families.

Platform: Python 3.12 / FastAPI / Pydantic v2 / LangGraph, PostgreSQL + Alembic, React 19 + Vite (four apps), Kotlin + Jetpack Compose, SSE, Docker on Render.

Key Features of the solution

  1. A readiness verdict, not a reminder. Come / Wait / Re-plan / Uncertain, with a date only when the chain is clear, and a forecast carrying its own coverage level instead of a confident guess.
  2. The whole network, asked automatically. Scheme desk, pharmacy, cab firm, hotel and lab each answer for themselves; a vendor signs in to its own screen and sees only its own jobs — a first name, a district, and a phone number only if the family agreed to share it.
  3. Affordability built into the plan. A railway patient-quota berth and a dharamshala for one household, an app cab and a hotel for another — and Saathi asks the family to confirm, with "I cannot manage any of these" always on the list, routed to a social worker rather than a cheaper booking.
  4. Four languages, and a voice. A plan a caregiver cannot read is not a plan.
  5. An audit trail that cannot be quietly edited. A hash-chained ledger that grows only when a fact actually changes.

Build Sprint Progress

The concept began as a reminder app: tell the family their appointment is coming. It became clear within the first week that the useful question is the opposite one — should they come at all — and the system was rebuilt around a readiness verdict. What shipped over the four weeks:

  • Weeks 1–2 — the decision core. Care-pathway dependency graph, conformal readiness intervals, the four-verdict planner, and the hash-chained ledger. The model was taken off the decision path entirely and given the single job of rendering.
  • Week 3 — the two products split. One screen serving both a navigator and a caregiver served neither, so the console and the family app became separate applications on separate devices. Four languages, read-aloud, and the guard on both directions of every message.
  • Week 4 — the network and the vendors. A directory of labs, pharmacies, cab firms, hotels and scheme desks that answer for themselves; a partner app so a vendor sees its own jobs and nothing about anybody's care; onboarding in seven stages; Sarvam speech on web and Android; deployment to Render.
  • Late addition — affordability. Onboarding had collected a ration card and an income band since the first commit and nothing read either, so every family was offered the same app cab and the same hotel. Cost tiers, means-aware selection and the family-facing budget conversation were built once that was noticed.

User Validation and AI Evaluation

No real patient data has been used anywhere, and there has been no clinical user testing. Saying otherwise would be the easiest thing in this document to get wrong.

Evaluation method. 50 synthetic families are generated with planted ground truth — the true date each pending report actually lands — and Saathi's verdicts are scored against the date the hospital's default policy would have given. Reproducible via python -m saathi.demo:

Wasted trips under the hospital default30 / 50 (60%)
Wasted trips with Saathi0 / 50 (0%)
Non-medical cost avoided (₹8,500 per trip)₹255,000
Evidence ledger intactyes

Limitations. Synthetic turnaround distributions are drawn from published queue behaviour, not from one hospital's real data; the per-trip cost is a share of a published mean, not measured spend. A pilot replaces both. Improvements made from findings: driving the real deployment rather than the test suite found a rendering enum that had silently diverted every re-plan message, a rate-limit stall that took the first answer from 226s to 43s, and a confirmation endpoint that returned 500 on the exact configuration the demo runs. Each is now pinned by a test.

Safety and Responsible AI

  • The model never decides whether a family travels. A deterministic planner decides; the model renders. The response schema's verdict enum is derived from the Decision type, so a new verdict cannot silently fall outside it.
  • Report content never enters the system. The ABDM ingest drops conclusion, result and presentedForm, and the pointer type has no field that could hold a finding. Saathi cannot leak what it does not store.
  • Clinical questions go to a human, always. A guard screens every inbound message and every outgoing one; a diverted message reaches the family as "a person is calling you", never as empty words.
  • Human oversight at the points that matter. A re-plan touching treatment or dose timing requires a doctor; a vendor is chosen by a person, not the system; a family's budget answer is a proposal until a navigator confirms it.
  • Privacy and consent. Consent per purpose, withdrawable, checked at every read and logged with refusals; a vendor sees a first name and a district, and a phone number only if the family agreed to share it. DPDP Act 2023 / ABDM consent model.
  • Honest uncertainty. No score anywhere. A forecast carries its coverage level and refuses to answer on thin calibration; when the system does not know, the verdict is Uncertain and a person is told.
  • Credential-free tests. All 631 tests run with every API key blanked, so no suite run can quietly depend on a live service or send real traffic.

Deployment and Sustainability Plan

Setting. A tertiary cancer centre's patient-navigation desk, alongside the existing HMIS rather than replacing it. Saathi reads status and writes nothing clinical, so it integrates at the thinnest possible seam: ABDM-linked status, a hospital appointment/report feed, and a directory of the vendors a journey already uses.

Integration requirements. ABDM OAuth and consent-manager routing (specified, not yet live); one hospital feed; an Institutional Ethics Committee approval and a named hospital partner before any real-data pilot.

Sustainability. The marginal cost per family is a handful of model calls and SMS; the saved cost is a ₹8,500 journey. Cheapest-adequate models are used deliberately (a 20B model for rendering), rate-limited and serialised. Running today on a container and a managed Postgres, deployable inside a hospital's own network with no change to the decision core.
